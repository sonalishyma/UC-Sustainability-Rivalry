The premise

Nine campuses, one competitive DNA

Every UC campus fights for the same things: the higher rank, the better recruiting class, the bigger name. That instinct is real, it is earned, and it is not a bad thing. It has simply never been pointed at the planet.

1983

The ranking era begins

On November 28, 1983, U.S. News & World Report published the first major United States college ranking list in its weekly news magazine. The feature helped revitalize the publication and eventually became its most profitable enterprise, though it did not literally save a dying daily newspaper.

Four decades later, we are redirecting that ranking instinct toward a subject that needs public attention. As the next generation shaping this planet, we can make sustainability visible, comparable, and worth competing over.

What the leaders have in common

Patterns worth adopting

Put sustainability in policy, not posters

Clear institutional commitments create accountability and guide long term environmental decisions. The top campuses codify their targets.

Build renewable capacity, especially solar

On site renewable generation cuts fossil fuel dependence and drives down campus greenhouse emissions. It is the single biggest lever in the emissions metric.

Engineer waste out of the system

Robust diversion programs minimize landfill use and turn recycling and reuse into default behavior rather than effort.

Teach it in the curriculum

Integrating climate literacy across programs means graduates carry sustainability into their careers. Engagement is a metric because it compounds.

Commit to net zero for the long term

Standing carbon neutrality commitments sustain efficiency and clean energy investment through leadership and budget cycles.

Methods & honesty notes

How the ranking works and where to squint

Method. Nine UC campuses were ranked from 1 to 9 on each of seven metrics from public data; the overall score is the unweighted sum of ranks, and the lowest total wins. It is simple by design: transparent, reproducible, and easy to argue with, which is the point of a ranking meant to spark competition.

Emissions is a composite proxy. The score combines Scope 1 to 3 emissions and renewable energy use per acre, using each campus's own 2023 sustainability report figures. It mixes units, so treat it as a rough intensity proxy that orders campuses, not a physical quantity. UCLA's small campus footprint of 419 acres is why its score is such an outlier.

STARS air & climate was normalized. Campuses reported under two different STARS versions, with sections worth 8 or 16 points, so scores were converted to percent of possible before ranking. That is why they are comparable.

Targets are set by each campus. The energy and water metrics measure each campus against its own goal; some 2025 water goals were approximated where no exact figure was published, as marked in the grid tooltips. The master ranking used absolute differences; this site displays percentages for comparison across campuses.

The framing is ours. "The Other Rivalry" is an editorial lens added to the original research. It is a way to make nine campuses take a real, sourced sustainability comparison as seriously as they take rank, football, and admit rates. The underlying data and methodology are unchanged from the original study.
